# Resume credibility anti-patterns

Use this reference to locate claims that need clarification or repair. Style alone is not proof of dishonesty.

## Evidence standard

Separate:

1. **Contradiction**: sourced facts cannot all be true, or chronology is impossible.
2. **Plausibility risk**: the claim is possible but unusually broad, fast, large, or senior.
3. **Evidence gap**: scope, method, baseline, attribution, or proof is missing.
4. **Mismatch**: the experience is credible but does not satisfy the target.
5. **Editorial weakness**: vague, repetitive, badly structured, or poorly targeted writing.

Stop a contradictory central claim. Require corroboration before strengthening a high-risk claim. Repair evidence gaps conservatively. Do not turn mismatch or style into an integrity judgment.

## Common anti-patterns

### Ownership inflation

Signals include repeated `led`, `owned end-to-end`, or organization-wide impact without a bounded component, decision right, team context, or duration. Separate the personal action from the team result and use the accurate ownership verb.

### Workload and chronology mismatch

Check overlapping full-time roles, future achievements, reversed dates, unexplained concurrent projects, and a short project claiming research, data, design, implementation, deployment, operations, and business adoption by one person.

### Metric laundering

Do not convert calls, users, traffic, documents, followers, awards, stars, code volume, or activity counts into quality, causality, user outcome, or business impact. Require a definition, unit, denominator, window, baseline or final attainment, conditions, attribution, and guardrail where relevant.

### Quantification theater

A percentage in nearly every bullet is a warning when samples, baselines, windows, and measurement paths are absent. Remove an indefensible number instead of inventing a benchmark.

### Tutorial or open-source repackaging

Label learning work, adaptation, or open-source modification honestly. Describe the actual delta, user, constraint, evaluation, failure handling, and accepted contribution. Do not rename public tutorial features as proprietary production work.

### Architecture without a job to do

A component list does not explain value. Require a user, task, unmet need, constraint, design decision, and validation path before presenting architecture depth.

### Production-depth theater

Do not use `enterprise-grade`, `production-ready`, `high concurrency`, or similar labels when deployment, monitoring, failure handling, security, governance, cost, rollout, and real operation are absent. Select only lifecycle signals relevant to the project.

### Skill and keyword inflation

Every retained skill should be `proven`, `used`, or clearly adjacent. Remove claimed-only and irrelevant items. Do not mirror a job description's tools, titles, industries, or methods without evidence.

### AI-generated-code theater

The share of AI-generated code is an input, not impact. Ask who defined requirements and architecture, how output was reviewed and tested, what rework occurred, and what user or operating result changed.

### Legacy-template lock-in

A rewrite that preserves the old order, project grouping, bullet boundaries, and space allocation may preserve the old strategic mistakes. Treat the old resume as evidence, then rebuild hierarchy around the target.

### Mechanically generated voice

Repeated cadence, identical subheadings, uniform bullet length, keyword stitching, and perfectly complete stories reduce distinctiveness. Restore concrete incidents, natural asymmetry, uncertainty, and wording the user can explain.

## Repair protocol

For each issue:

1. locate the claim without reproducing unnecessary personal identifiers;
2. classify the problem and confidence;
3. explain why it matters;
4. ask one answerable verification question;
5. substantiate, narrow, reattribute, redate, relabel, revise, or remove;
6. ensure the repaired claim is no stronger than the evidence.
