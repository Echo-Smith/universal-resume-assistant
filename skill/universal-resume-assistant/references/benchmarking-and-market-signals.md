# Benchmarking and market signals

Use this reference when comparing a resume with another candidate, public profile, career article, tutorial project, template, or current job-description set.

## Classify the source

| Source | Useful signal | Main caution |
|---|---|---|
| Real candidate resume | Positioning and stated evidence | Claims may be unverified |
| Anonymized hiring case | Recruiter response to a profile | Channel and selection context may be missing |
| Teaching template | Structure and coverage | Claims may be illustrative |
| Tutorial-project resume | Mapping features to job language | Many candidates may share it |
| Marketing persona | Attention and positioning | Not a fair ordinary-candidate baseline |
| Public expert profile | External credibility and career signals | Reputation may carry weak prose |
| Career article | A hiring thesis or heuristic | One author's view is not market-wide evidence |
| Live job-description set | Current requirements | One role does not represent the market |

State what was verified and what remains author-asserted or screenshot-only.

## Separate three judgments

### Document quality

Assess target clarity, relevance, ownership, evidence, metric integrity, structure, concision, consistency, and interview defensibility.

### Candidate competitiveness

Assess formal title, relevant tenure, employer or product credibility, operating scale, commercial or organizational outcomes, public work, external validation, and application channel.

### Role fit and funnel risk

Map hard requirements and transferable evidence. Identify whether the likely blocker is identity classification, title, tenure, domain, platform, scale, commercial proof, interview depth, location, employment type, or channel disadvantage.

Do not raise the document score because a candidate has a famous employer or public following. Do not assume a well-written resume can overcome a hard experience mismatch.

## Standard and distinctive signals

Standard signals reduce recruiter inference: expected title, relevant tenure, named responsibility, formal releases, team collaboration, operating scale, recognized platform, client delivery, and attributable outcomes.

Distinctive signals create upside: original work, memorable product judgment, public artifacts, rigorous evaluation, unusual domain bridges, or independent shipping evidence.

A non-standard candidate often needs both: preserve the distinctive evidence while adding enough standard signals for rapid classification.

## Interpret funnel events narrowly

- One cold application without a reply may mean unread, low priority, channel disadvantage, or screen mismatch.
- Recruiter conversation without a business interview suggests identity, title, tenure, or hard-requirement risk.
- Business interview rejection provides stronger evidence about depth, metrics, judgment, or fit.
- A repeated pattern across a defined sample justifies changing targeting or positioning more strongly than one event.

Recommend at most one useful follow-up. Add a relevant artifact, decision note, or result rather than repeatedly asking whether the resume was read.

## Extract lessons without imitation

Keep lessons that reduce inference and strengthen proof. Reject keyword accumulation, decorative percentages, tutorial work presented as original invention, reputation treated as proof of every claim, or another profession's evidence model copied without translation.

For a benchmark review, return:

1. source nature and evidence boundary;
2. document-quality assessment;
3. candidate signals outside that assessment;
4. reusable lessons and misleading patterns;
5. target-role comparison;
6. one to three high-value actions.
