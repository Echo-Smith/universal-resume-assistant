# Tailoring and writing guide

Use this reference to map a target role, reconstruct career evidence, draft bullets, or translate a resume.

## Requirement map

Separate a job description into:

- mandatory credentials or legal requirements;
- core responsibilities;
- domain knowledge;
- methods, tools, or craft skills;
- leadership and collaboration expectations;
- outcomes the employer appears to value;
- generic traits that should not drive keyword matching.

For each requirement, record evidence strength, source, risk, and the resume action. The action may be `lead`, `include`, `clarify`, `ask`, `omit`, or `label as transferable`.

## Profession-neutral evidence lenses

Choose only lenses relevant to the target.

| Lens | Useful evidence |
|---|---|
| Operations and administration | volume, cycle time, error rate, compliance, coordination, service level |
| Sales and customer work | territory or portfolio, conversion, retention, relationship depth, revenue attribution |
| Education and training | learner group, curriculum, assessment, progression, engagement, safeguarding |
| Healthcare and care work | licensed scope, caseload, safety, quality, patient experience, teamwork, confidentiality |
| Engineering and technology | constraints, design decisions, reliability, performance, adoption, maintainability |
| Research and analysis | question, method, dataset or corpus, rigor, finding, publication or decision impact |
| Creative and design work | brief, audience, creative decision, production role, selection, review, reach, portfolio proof |
| Finance and legal work | jurisdiction, controls, accuracy, risk, value, timeliness, confidentiality, credentialed scope |
| Public service and nonprofit work | population served, accessibility, policy delivery, stewardship, community outcome |
| Skilled trades and field work | qualification, environment, safety, quality, schedule, rework, equipment, client result |
| Leadership and management | team scope, decision rights, coaching, resource tradeoffs, change adoption, shared outcome |

Do not convert one profession's success model into a universal template. For example, revenue is not the default outcome for teaching, safety is not merely a metric, and publication count does not by itself establish research quality.

## Question design

Ask one to three high-value questions at a time. Prioritize questions that could change selection, credibility, seniority, or fit.

Useful prompts include:

- What was the starting condition or responsibility?
- What part was personally owned, and what belonged to the team?
- What judgment or method mattered most?
- How was the result observed, recorded, or evaluated?
- What scale, frequency, duration, or constraint makes the work understandable?
- Which detail is confidential or should be generalized?
- Which claim would a reference or artifact support?

Avoid asking for a number when qualitative evidence is the more honest result.

## Drafting patterns

Use patterns as diagnostics, not mandatory sentence templates.

- `responsibility + scope + distinguishing method`
- `starting condition + action + observed result`
- `risk or constraint + judgment + safeguarded outcome`
- `audience or client need + craft decision + reception or delivery`
- `transferable capability + source context + target relevance`

Vary sentence openings and length. A direct duty line can be stronger than a fabricated achievement arc.

## Metrics

Check:

- definition and unit;
- baseline and final value;
- time window;
- numerator and denominator for percentages;
- comparable conditions;
- measurement source;
- personal versus team attribution;
- whether the number is confidential.

If a precise metric is unavailable, use bounded qualitative evidence such as formal adoption, selection, repeat engagement, passed audit, completed delivery, published work, documented feedback, or expanded responsibility.

## Career changes and gaps

- Lead with the strongest transferable evidence and explain the bridge through actions, not slogans.
- Keep direct and adjacent experience distinct.
- Use a short neutral explanation only when it helps the reader understand chronology.
- Do not treat caregiving, health, study, unemployment, migration, or other gaps as evidence of low ability.
- Use projects, service, courses, licenses, portfolios, or placements only at their true level of maturity.

## Translation

- Preserve facts, scope, dates, and uncertainty.
- Adapt headings, date formats, punctuation, and terminology to the destination market.
- Keep credentials in their official form; explain an equivalent without claiming formal equivalence.
- Avoid literal translations of titles when they change seniority or regulated scope.
- Do not introduce keywords or stronger verbs during translation.
- Check names of institutions, qualifications, publications, and professional bodies against authoritative sources when accuracy is consequential.
