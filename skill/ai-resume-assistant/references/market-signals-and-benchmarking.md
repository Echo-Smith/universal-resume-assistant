# Market signals and resume benchmarking

Use this reference when the user compares their resume with another candidate, a public expert, a career article, a tutorial project, a “perfect resume,” or a recruiting-market example.

## Classify the source

Before comparison, label the artifact:

| Source type | What it can show | Main caution |
|---|---|---|
| Real candidate resume | Actual positioning and evidence claims | Facts and outcomes may still be unverified |
| Anonymized hiring case | Recruiter response to a real profile | Selection context and channel may be missing |
| Teaching template | Useful structure and keyword coverage | Often contains idealized or illustrative claims |
| Tutorial-project resume | How a course maps features to job language | Many applicants may share the same project and wording |
| Marketing persona | Attention and product-marketing strategy | Not a fair baseline for an ordinary applicant |
| Public expert profile | Strong external credibility and career signals | Reputation may carry the resume more than the prose |
| Career article | Author's hiring thesis and heuristics | Advice is not automatically market-wide evidence |
| Live JD set | Current employer requirements | One JD does not represent the whole market |

Use public repositories, product pages, employer records, or other primary artifacts when factual verification materially affects the conclusion. State what remains screenshot-only or author-asserted.

## Separate the three judgments

### 1. Document quality

Score only what the resume communicates: target clarity, relevance, ownership, technical or product depth, impact, metric integrity, structure, concision, consistency, and interview defensibility.

### 2. Candidate competitiveness

Assess separately:

- formal target-role title and relevant tenure;
- recognized employer, shipped product, or client credibility;
- user or traffic scale and operating responsibility;
- commercial, organizational, or adoption outcomes;
- public work, repository history, independent usage, awards, or references;
- ability to enter through referral, direct outreach, or public reputation.

These signals can win interviews even when the resume is dense or imperfect. They must not inflate the document score.

### 3. Role fit and funnel risk

Map hard requirements and transferable evidence. Identify whether the likely blocker is:

- ATS or recruiter identity classification;
- formal experience or title;
- missing domain, platform, scale, or commercial proof;
- business-interview depth;
- team or stakeholder evidence;
- compensation, location, or employment type;
- application-channel disadvantage.

## Identify standard and distinctive signals

**Standard signals** reduce the recruiter's inference burden: expected title, relevant tenure, named responsibility, formal releases, team collaboration, production scale, recognizable platform, client delivery, and commercial results.

**Distinctive signals** create upside: an original project, unusual product judgment, a public artifact, rigorous evaluation, a memorable tradeoff, a strong domain bridge, or evidence that the candidate can independently ship.

A candidate may be strong on one and weak on the other. State the combination explicitly. Do not tell a non-standard candidate to erase their distinctiveness; help them add enough standard signals that the recruiter can classify them quickly.

Run the first-screen test:

1. target role and seniority are visible;
2. relevant experience type is honest;
3. strongest product or project proof appears early;
4. portfolio or public artifact is easy to access;
5. a transition does not require the recruiter to infer the bridge alone.

## Evaluate external proof

Use this rough evidence ladder, adjusted for the role:

1. claim only;
2. screenshot, document, or reproducible local result;
3. accessible product or repository with coherent history;
4. real users, adoption, feedback, or repeated operation;
5. independent validation such as accepted contribution, award, reference, or public usage;
6. organizational scale, commercial outcome, or sustained production responsibility.

Do not collapse followers, stars, users, traffic, revenue, and quality into one concept. Each proves something different.

For AI projects, public deployment helps establish existence but not ownership or product value by itself. Tutorial origin lowers distinctiveness unless the candidate shows a meaningful delta, user, dataset, constraint, evaluation, or operating result.

## Read silence and rejection carefully

One rejection or silent recruiter is evidence about one funnel event, not the entire market. Report the narrow inference:

- no reply after a cold application: likely low priority, unread, or screen mismatch;
- recruiter conversation without business interview: likely identity, tenure, title, or hard-requirement gap;
- business interview failure: likely depth, metrics, judgment, or role-fit gap;
- repeated pattern across a defined sample: stronger evidence for changing targeting or positioning.

Recommend at most one value-adding follow-up when appropriate. A follow-up should add a relevant artifact, decision note, or result, not ask repeatedly whether the resume was read.

## Extract lessons without imitation

Preserve reusable principles:

- make the target and strongest proof obvious in the first third;
- provide accessible work and external evidence;
- show decisions, shipped behavior, operating responsibility, and results;
- select one to three complementary projects;
- tailor evidence to the JD;
- support personal advantages with proof.

Reject misleading patterns:

- keyword accumulation without decisions or task context;
- a percentage in every bullet;
- tutorial work described as independent invention;
- public reputation treated as proof of every claim;
- AI-generated-code share treated as business value;
- another profession's project mix copied into the user's role;
- an idealized sample treated as the minimum market standard.

Translate the lesson into the user's role. A product candidate usually needs stronger user, prioritization, interaction, experiment, collaboration, and business evidence than a backend engineer; an engineer usually needs deeper implementation, reliability, and production evidence than a product candidate.

## Recommended output

For a benchmark comparison, return:

1. source nature and what was actually verified;
2. 40-point document-quality score;
3. candidate signals outside the score;
4. strongest reusable lessons;
5. patterns not to copy;
6. a comparison with the user's target role;
7. one to three high-value next actions.
